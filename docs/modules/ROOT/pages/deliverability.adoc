= Deliverability

Five record types decide whether your mail is accepted and whether anyone can
forge it. mailctl publishes all of them from one block, and enforces the rules that
are easy to get wrong by hand.

== SPF: exactly one record

RFC 7208 permits exactly one SPF TXT record per name. Two is not "more coverage" —
it is a permanent error that some receivers treat as a hard fail.

That is why no provider publishes its own SPF record directly. Each contributes its
requirement, and mailctl merges them:

[source,yaml]
----
deliverability:
  spfIncludes:
    - include:servers.mailgun.org
----

With Purelymail inbound and that extra include, the published record is one TXT:

----
v=spf1 include:_spf.purelymail.com include:servers.mailgun.org ~all
----

Two rules the merge enforces:

* **The strictest qualifier wins.** If any input asks for `-all`, the result is
`-all`. Loosening a policy silently is the worse mistake.
* **Entries are single mechanisms.** No whitespace, no `all` qualifier, no
`redirect=`. A `spfIncludes` entry that could override the record's qualifier is
rejected, because the whole point of merging is that mailctl controls the outcome.

mailctl also rejects a malformed mechanism outright — a bare `include:` with no
value, or `include` with no colon. Both produce a record that looks configured and
fails only at other people's receiving servers, which is the worst kind of broken.

== DMARC

[source,yaml]
----
deliverability:
  dmarc:
    policy: quarantine        # none | quarantine | reject
    subdomainPolicy: reject
    pct: 100
    rua: mailto:dmarc@example.com
----

Start at `none` with a `rua` address, read the reports for a week or two, and only
then move to `quarantine` and `reject`. Going straight to `reject` on a domain whose
senders you have not yet enumerated silently discards legitimate mail.

Setting `dmarc` makes mailctl the owner of the `_dmarc` name. Providers that would
otherwise publish their own `_dmarc` CNAME step aside, so the two never compete.

== MTA-STS

MTA-STS tells sending servers to require TLS for your domain. It needs a TXT record
*and* a policy file served over HTTPS at a well-known URL.

[source,yaml]
----
deliverability:
  mtaSts:
    mode: testing             # none | testing | enforce
    maxAge: 604800
    deploy: true
----

`deploy: true` has mailctl generate and upload a Cloudflare Worker that serves the
policy, and bind it to `mta-sts.<domain>`. No Node toolchain or Wrangler install is
involved — the Worker is generated and uploaded directly.

[WARNING]
====
Go to `testing` first and watch your TLS-RPT reports for a week. In `enforce` mode a
misconfigured MX becomes *undeliverable mail with no warning* — senders will refuse
to deliver rather than fall back.
====

The policy id is derived from the policy's content, so an unchanged policy keeps its
id and an edited one gets a new one automatically. `mode: none` withdraws the policy
rather than being a no-op.

== TLS-RPT

[source,yaml]
----
deliverability:
  tlsRpt: mailto:tls-reports@example.com
----

Asks receivers to report TLS delivery failures. Worth enabling *before* MTA-STS, so
you have reporting in place while the policy is in `testing`. Each comma-separated
address must begin with `mailto:` or `https:`.

== BIMI

[source,yaml]
----
deliverability:
  bimi:
    logo: https://example.com/logo.svg
----

Publishes the BIMI record pointing at your logo. Note that most receivers also
require a Verified Mark Certificate and a DMARC policy of `quarantine` or `reject`
before they will display it, so BIMI on its own is unlikely to show anything.

== A sensible order

. Get mail working: MX, SPF, DKIM. Confirm with `mailctl audit`.
. Add DMARC at `policy: none` with a `rua` address.
. Add `tlsRpt`.
. Read the reports. Fix whatever they surface.
. Move DMARC to `quarantine`, then `reject`.
. Add MTA-STS at `mode: testing`.
. After a quiet week, move MTA-STS to `enforce`.

Each step is reversible until the last two, which is exactly why they come last.
